Course Title: Theory of Computation — Chapter 1: Automata, The Methods and the Madness
Course Code: TOC-CORE-101
Level: Undergraduate / 3rd semester Computer Science
Prerequisites: Discrete Mathematics, Introductory Programming

Course Description:
This unit introduces the foundational concepts of automata theory, situating
them within the broader landscape of theoretical computer science. The
emphasis is on the *methods* (proof techniques, formal languages, finite
state systems) and the *madness* (why computability, complexity, and
intractability matter for working software engineers, not just theorists).
The unit is the opening chapter of Hopcroft, Motwani & Ullman's
"Introduction to Automata Theory, Languages, and Computation" and
sets the stage for the rest of the course.

Learning Objectives:
By the end of this unit, students will be able to:
  1. Articulate why the study of automata and complexity is part of
     the core of computer science (software for digital circuits,
     lexical analyzers, text search, protocol verification).
  2. Recognise the central concepts that recur throughout the book:
     alphabet, string, language, deterministic vs nondeterministic
     systems, the trade-off between expressiveness and analyzability.
  3. Apply the standard proof techniques used in this field:
     deductive proofs, reformulation, proof by contradiction, proof
     by induction (simple, structural, and mutual), pigeonhole
     principle.
  4. Reason informally about finite automata: states, transitions,
     acceptors, the relationship between a system and its abstract
     state machine.
  5. Distinguish tractable from intractable problems at an intuitive
     level, and explain why this matters in practice.

Unit Outline:
  1. Why Study Automata Theory
     1.1 Introduction
     1.2 The central role of finite automata in hardware and software
         (digital circuits, lexical analyzers, text search, protocol
         verification)
     1.3 Why this book is structured the way it is
  2. Introduction to Finite Automata
     2.1 An informal picture: a system with a finite number of states
     2.2 The two basic finite-state models: deterministic and
         nondeterministic
     2.3 Finite automata in real systems: elevators, vending machines,
         traffic lights
  3. Structural Representations
     3.1 Transition graphs
     3.2 Transition tables
     3.3 Trade-offs and when to use which representation
  4. Automata and Complexity
     4.1 Decidability vs undecidability (preview)
     4.2 Tractable vs intractable problems
     4.3 The role of automata theory in the working programmer's life
  5. Languages, Strings, and Alphabets
     5.1 Alphabets and strings
     5.2 Languages as sets of strings
     5.3 Operations on languages
  6. The Central Concepts of Automata Theory (Part 1)
     6.1 Deductive proofs
     6.2 Proofs by contradiction
     6.3 Proofs by induction (simple induction)
  7. The Central Concepts of Automata Theory (Part 2)
     7.1 Structural induction
     7.2 Mutual induction
     7.3 The pigeonhole principle and its consequences
  8. Exercises and Gradience Problems
     8.1 Routine exercises
     8.2 Gradience problems (extended reasoning)

Assessment Plan:
  - Weekly problem set (proof technique focus): 30%
  - Midterm covering Sections 1-5: 25%
  - Term project: design a finite-state model for a real system of
    the student's choice (elevator, vending machine, protocol, etc.):
    25%
  - Final exam (full chapter): 20%

Reference Material:
  Primary: Hopcroft, Motwani, Ullman — "Introduction to Automata
    Theory, Languages, and Computation" (3rd ed.), Chapter 1.
  Secondary: Sipser — "Introduction to the Theory of Computation",
    Chapters 0-1.
